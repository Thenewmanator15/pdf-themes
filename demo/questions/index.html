01

My PDF app already has a dark mode. Why isn't that enough?

Because it has to guess, and guessing can go wrong on anything whose colours carry meaning.

A PDF says which colour to paint, but not what a colour means. A PDF app can tell text from a picture, but not whether a blue line is a data series, a highlight or a brand colour, nor whether a picture is a photo or a diagram. So a PDF app that offers a dark mode treats colours by rule: some invert every colour, some invert only lightness, and some swap in their own colours. Black text on white survives that. Colours that carry meaning don't. A plain inversion turns a blue line orange and an orange one blue, so a caption that says "the blue line" points at the wrong line. It turns a colour scale into a different one, a photo into a negative, and anything drawn on a dark background light. A chart's key is inverted with its lines, so it still matches them. Inverting only lightness keeps hues, but still flips a colour scale's light and dark ends.

Some people with low vision rely on settings like these, and in two studies they caused trouble with charts. In interviews with 11 legally blind adults who still have some sight, inverting didn't always give the result people wanted, or the same result for everyone: one participant's bar chart didn't invert as he expected and came across in very light grey, and one chart still fell short of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) once a participant's filter had inverted it. In another study, with 22 people with low vision, 5 used colour filters and 3 used colour themes during its tasks. The chart aids being tested mostly worked with people's own tools, and where they didn't, filters and themes were the most common reason. That study didn't test themes, dark mode or PDF. In a small study of 12 people with typical sight, inverted charts were read with no measurable loss of accuracy or speed, but their colours were rated far less like the originals than those of a designed dark version. So the main loss is what the colours mean. With a theme that the author made and checked, nothing has to be guessed.

More: what recolouring does, with Wang, Zhao and Kim 2024, Sechayk et al. 2026 and Karunathilaka et al. 2025; and the demo, whose inverted and recoloured views are imitations made by this site, not screenshots of a PDF app.

02

Isn't dark mode better for everyone?

No. On average, people with typical sight proofread better with dark text on light, most of all when the text is small.

They also saw fine detail better. An earlier study from the same lab found that the advantage comes from the brighter screen, not from which way round the colours are, though it used low-contrast text in a dark room. In a near-dark room, light text on dark took longer to read at a glance; in bright light it didn't.

Light text on dark does help some people with low vision read faster, more often those whose eyes scatter light, as with cataract, but one study of children, known only from its abstract, found no clinical test that predicts who. In a survey of 133 adults with low vision, 46% said they preferred white text on black and 39% black on white. And when 104 people did chart tasks in light and in dark, there was no measurable difference on average. Their preferences split by age: of the 52 under 60, 24 preferred dark and 14 light, and of the 52 aged 60 or over, 23 preferred light and 6 dark; the rest had no preference or weren't sure. So light stays the default, and dark is a choice for the people it helps or who prefer it.

More: light and dark, with Piepenbrock et al. 2013 and 2014, Dobres et al. 2017, Wu et al. 2020 and While and Sarvghad 2024.

03

Do coloured pages help with dyslexia?

Some people say a tinted page is more comfortable, though whether that is more than a placebo effect is disputed. Colour doesn't treat dyslexia.

In one large study of screen backgrounds, 341 adults, 89 of them with dyslexia or at risk of it, read faster on peach, orange and yellow than on blue, blue grey and green. It didn't test a white page, so it can't say whether a tint beats white, and the authors say this doesn't necessarily mean warm colours are recommended. An eye-tracking study of 92 people, half with dyslexia, found no effect of colour. The British Dyslexia Association's style guide suggests dark text on a light, not white, background, such as cream or a soft pastel.

Two systematic reviews of coloured overlays disagree. One found that their use for reading difficulties "cannot be endorsed", and put the benefits people report down to placebo, practice or the effect of being studied. The other, by authors who work with one tinting system, found weak evidence that filters chosen for each person can ease symptoms or speed up reading in people with visual stress, and called for larger trials. Visual stress is discomfort when reading, such as eye strain or headaches; that review suggests about one in five people with dyslexia may have it. So the tints are offered for comfort and choice, not as a treatment.

The four tints here are a starting point. I found no standard colours for reading tints. The ones on this site are the project's own, paler than the colours tested with readers, and this site's turquoise is a pale blue, near the cool colours that read slowest in the large study. (The turquoise the tool works out for a finished PDF is a true pale turquoise.) The tested colours are on the evidence page, and which tints to offer is still open. Nobody with dyslexia or visual stress has tried these tints yet.

More: tints and dyslexia, with Rello and Bigham 2017, Rello and Baeza-Yates 2017, Griffiths et al. 2016, Evans and Allen 2016 and the British Dyslexia Association's guide.

04

Does it make files bigger?

For themes built from the source, a little: mostly only the colours are stored again. Themes worked out for a finished PDF can cost more.

A page of plots grew 4.3% with all eight themes, from 38,318 to 39,966 bytes. The proposal, about 0.75 MB, grew about 0.1% with its seven extra themes, since most of its size is pictures that every theme shares. Putting a light and a dark version in one file as two layers, which an author can do today, made the same page of plots 41% bigger, and a simpler test page 2.7% bigger. Pictures that change between themes cost more: on a test document built to be hard, where two pictures change, its light and dark themes added 38%, against 43% for the same two as layers.

When the tool works themes out for a finished PDF, the cost varies more. In a test of the development version of 17 October 2026 on 298 public PDFs, each compared with its original saved the same way, the median file grew 0.1% and three in four grew by less than 1%, but 32 grew by more than a quarter. Printed web pages grew by a median of 46%, and one 21-page printed web page with no pictures tripled, from 189 KB to 565 KB. The tool also saves files more compactly, so against the files as published most got slightly smaller, and that web page, 545 KB as published, grew about 4%. Giving each picture its own recoloured copy for every theme was tried and dropped, because files grew up to ten times: a scanned book went from 25 MB to 179 MB. So the tool doesn't give pictures a copy for each theme.

05

Will it break anything in PDF apps that don't know about themes?

It shouldn't. They show the default colours, and in the tests so far nearly every file looked as before.

The themes sit where those apps don't look. In a test of the development version of 17 October 2026, which isn't on GitHub yet, the tool added themes to 298 public PDFs of up to 40 pages. Drawn before and after in PDFium, the engine in Chrome, at 72 dpi, 266 looked identical and 31 differed by at most one step out of 255 in a colour value. The other one differed through PDFium's own handling of a Display P3 colour. The tool refused one more file, a report whose pages use a font missing from the file. As first found, on 11 October with an earlier version, 7 files changed and 14 took too long to theme; after fixes to the tool, none took too long and the Display P3 file was the only change left. In a sweep on 14 October, also with an earlier version, MuPDF drew 17 files differently where pictures are stored as palettes, by up to 125 steps in places, because it shrinks such pictures differently from the same pictures in full colour (a MuPDF quirk); it drew 26 more within five steps. Why 31 files differ by one step isn't yet known, and Acrobat, Apple Preview and pdf.js, the viewer in Firefox, haven't been checked across the set.

Picking a theme changes no bytes in the file, so the text and the tags that screen readers use stay as they are. The 31 files in the set that pass the veraPDF validator's checks for the PDF/A archive standard or the PDF/UA accessibility standard still passed once themed, checked as stored. veraPDF doesn't reach the other themes, so those weren't checked one by one. As first found, with a version of 14 October, 7 of these files failed; two faults were then fixed. Signatures need care. A signature made after the themes went in covers every theme, but adding themes rewrites a file, so it has to happen before signing. A theme could also hide text by painting it in the paper colour, so the proposal asks signature checkers to draw every theme and say which is shown.

06

Who has to do the work?

Mostly the author, once: picking each theme's colours. pdf-themes merges the versions into one file.

For a Typst document, one pdf-themes command builds it in each theme and merges the builds. Versions made with other tools can be merged as well. The versions have to draw the same text and shapes and differ only in colour, so a template may need small changes first: if one theme draws a border or a gradient, every theme has to, even where it can't be seen, and a page that prints its own theme's name won't merge. Tools that already name their colours, such as style sheets and the theme colours in office software, could in principle write themes directly. LaTeX, word processors and plotting libraries don't do so yet.

For a PDF with no source, the tool can work themes out from the file's own colours, mostly by turning light surfaces dark and dark text light while keeping hues, much as some PDF apps do. Like them, it can't tell what a colour means. In a test of the development version of 17 October 2026 on 298 public PDFs, 180 kept all eight themes, 13 of them only because they had no text to check. By kind: printed web pages 27 of 27, Typst documents 11 of 15, LaTeX documents 61 of 82, reports 62 of 99, test files 8 of 15, slides 6 of 30 and scans 5 of 30. As first found, on 13 October with an earlier version, 163 kept all eight. Worked-out themes can also fail on a long file: on a 31-page test file, all six themes the tool worked out failed their check, though on its first page alone all six passed. So themes made by the author come first.

More: the tool, and the public PDFs.

07

Why not let me pick any colours I like?

You should be able to, and themes don't take that away.

WCAG's highest level asks that people can choose their own colours for text and its background, and says a browser or other app can provide the means. Under the proposal, a person's own settings, such as a Windows contrast theme or Acrobat's Replace Document Colors, still come first. But only the author knows what the colours mean. Inverting changes a chart's key along with its lines, so the key still matches, but a caption that says "the blue line" then points at the wrong one. A theme the author made can keep each colour meaning what the key and caption say, and the tool checks its text contrast before it goes in. Your own settings stay.

08

Can a theme make thin lines thicker for people with low vision?

Not yet. In a file set up for it, three PDF engines would already draw it, but the proposal keeps themes to colour for now.

Thicker lines make colour differences easier to see, at least for people with typical vision. In one study, all 11 people with low vision commented on chart grid lines and tick marks, mainly on how thin, pale and sparse they were; two asked for bolder lines and one for larger tick marks. In a test file whose line width came from a setting a theme can swap, PDFium, MuPDF and Poppler drew the line at 3 pt instead of 0.5 pt when the theme was shown, and did the same for a dash pattern. But most tools set line widths directly in the page content, where a theme can't reach them, and I found no study that measures whether heavier lines help people with low vision read charts. So the proposal keeps themes to colour and lists line width as an open question. On a web page it is easy: the demo draws heavier lines and grid in the more contrast themes.

More: lines, dashes and grids, and the test in three PDF engines.

09

What should I do with my own figures?

Save them as vector graphics with colours for each theme, and don't rely on colour alone.

A figure drawn as lines and text (SVG or PDF) can follow the theme, as long as your tools draw it in each theme's colours when they build that theme. A picture of a chart, such as a PNG, follows the theme only if you supply a version for each theme; otherwise it stays a white box on a dark page. When the tool works themes out for a finished PDF, it can recolour a simple picture on white with few colours, but it leaves photos and detailed pictures as they are. A grey scan of a page is the exception: its dark themes turn the whole page light on dark, a photo on it included.

Give every line or group a marker, a dash pattern or a label as well as a colour. When a crowded display was shown for about a tenth of a second, people picked out a group far more accurately when it differed in both colour and shape than in either alone. WCAG also asks that colour isn't the only way information is given, and a second cue survives a PDF app's recolouring.

For a colour scale, use one that changes steadily in lightness, such as viridis or cividis, rather than a rainbow, which cost medical students accuracy in one study. Viridis and cividis were designed with simulations of colour vision deficiency, though I found no test of them with colour-blind readers. A colour scale shows data, so it keeps its colours in every theme, with a frame where its darkest colours would meet a dark page. In one study of maps, a dark-mode version whose darkest colours nearly matched the background left people unsure in a third of their answers about which way the scale ran. Make the text big enough once the figure is printed at its final size: clear print guidance asks for at least 12 pt, and ideally 14 pt, though I found no study of label size in charts. In one study of map labels on a screen, which I know only from its abstract and the authors' summary, young students' eye movements suggested they searched most efficiently at the middle of three sizes. In a study of older adults using interactive charts online, some found some of the text too small. Keep labels as real text, not converted to shapes, so they can be searched and their contrast can be checked.

More: figures, with Nothelfer et al. 2017, Borkin et al. 2011 and Schiewe 2024; and text and labels.

10

Is there a theme for colour blindness?

No. Colour blindness has to be handled when the figure is made.

About 8% of men and 0.5% of women have some colour vision deficiency, or colour blindness, by the usual estimate. Themes don't fix it. Someone with it may read in any theme, so a figure's colours have to be chosen for them in every theme, with a marker, dash or label on every line as well. In one study, which I know only from its abstract, map readers with red-green deficiency were as accurate as others when the colours were chosen with them in mind, though still slower, and they overwhelmingly preferred those maps. When 1,500 figures from visualisation papers were each shown through four simulations of colour vision deficiency, most raters judged about 60% of the simulated images accessible, a third borderline or undecided and 7% not. But in another study, 9 of 12 colour-blind people interviewed disputed the simulations. Changing colours for a theme can also bring two lines closer together, and pdf-themes doesn't yet check for colour vision deficiency.

More: colour vision deficiency, with Crameri et al. 2020, Olson and Brewer 1997, Angerbauer et al. 2022 and Jiang, Arunkumar and Bryan 2026.

11

Don't journals already check figures?

Not in a way that reaches every figure. Some journals leave a figure's quality to its author, and audits of published figures find them uneven.

PLOS, for example, says the "final quality of figures is the responsibility of the author", takes only TIFF or EPS files, and says nothing about colour vision deficiency. Elsevier recommends figures that people with impaired colour vision can read, and a study of psychology journals found their author guidelines rarely mentioned colour vision deficiency. In 580 papers from the top journals in three fields, few met every good-practice criterion for their images (2% to 16%), and 21% to 45% had some images that readers with a common colour vision deficiency couldn't make out, judged with a simulation. Of 4,964 images from one journal checked by hand, 12.8% would be hard for such readers, and that share fell between 2012 and 2021. Of 20,000 paper PDFs, 3.2% met six basic accessibility checks, and only 5 described their figures meaningfully for screen readers. In my own run over public PDFs, 39 of 82 arXiv papers had some text below the WCAG contrast minimum in their authors' own colours, as the tool measures it.

A figure supplied as a TIFF is a picture: its text can't be searched or read aloud, and it can't follow a theme. The more a shared pipeline settles and checks when the document is built, such as colours, a second cue and text size, the less depends on each author. That is my inference; nobody has tested such a pipeline.

More: figures as journals publish them, with Jambor et al. 2021, Stevens et al. 2024 and Kumar and Wang 2024; and what I measured.

12

Does a themed PDF meet accessibility rules?

Themes don't make a PDF accessible. They leave its tags and reading order alone, and each theme's text contrast is measured before it goes in.

Whether a PDF is accessible under PDF/UA, the accessibility standard for PDF, depends on things such as tags, reading order and alternative text for pictures, and themes leave those alone. The proposal on this site, with all eight themes in it, passes veraPDF's automated checks for PDF/UA-1, and for the PDF/A-2a archive standard, in every theme. PDF/UA also needs checks by a person, which a validator can't make.

PDF/UA's own tests set no contrast ratio, so the tool uses WCAG's. Before a theme goes in, the tool measures each piece of text against the background in its box. WCAG asks for at least 4.5:1 for ordinary text and 3:1 for large text, and 7:1 and 4.5:1 at its highest level, which the more contrast themes are checked against. A theme the author made that falls short is still written, without the mark in the file that says it passed, and the author gets a list of the text that fell short. A theme the tool works out is left out if its text still falls short after the tool's own fixes. Lines and shapes below 3:1 are listed for the author, but don't decide the mark. The check has known limits, not yet fixed: it takes text size from the font size as written, so text in a figure drawn at half size can be held to the large-text minimum (3:1 instead of 4.5:1), and where text crosses two backgrounds it is measured against the one it contrasts with best. WCAG's contrast rules don't ask for themes; its highest level asks that people can choose their own colours for text and its background, which a PDF app can provide.

13

Has anyone shown that themes help?

Not for themed PDFs. The studies show that people prefer different colours, and that some read better in them, not that themes are the best way to give them.

The studies on the evidence page tested colours on screens, on paper and through lenses, and many of them are small. They show that preferences differ, that light text on dark helps some people with low vision read faster, and that inverting, which some people rely on, can be unreliable on charts. Nobody with low vision, dyslexia or visual stress has tried these themes yet, and my own measurements are of files and software, not of people. The most direct test would have people with low vision read charts in the author's themes and with inverting, and compare accuracy and time as well as which they preferred. It would need a university to lead it.

14

Is this a standard? Can I use it now?

Not yet. It is a working prototype for a proposal to the PDF Association. You can try it in the viewer on this site, the only app that can switch themes so far.

The viewer opens only the documents on this site, not your own PDFs. Every other PDF app shows a themed file in its default colours. The code and the draft specification are on GitHub, but the tool isn't yet a released package you can install, and its latest fixes are in a development version that isn't on GitHub yet. The themes sit under a temporary name in the file until the PDF Association agrees one. The proposal, written for its working group, is the first document in the viewer. A request for dark mode in PDF has been open on the Association's public tracker since November 2023, and I posted pdf-themes there in October 2026 (the request). Nobody with low vision, dyslexia or visual stress has tried the themes yet.
