What is known about colours, themes and readable figures

This page gathers what published studies and standards say about reading in light, dark, tinted and higher contrast colours, about what happens when a reader's software recolours a page, and about making charts that more people can read. It adds what I have measured with pdf-themes. Each finding is marked by how strong it is.

Established

Read in full, and resting on a peer-reviewed study of 30 or more people, on several studies, on a systematic review or on a standard's own text. Stated within its limits.

Established, with limits

Read in full, but resting on one small study or a weaker source, such as a thesis or a preprint. In the tables it reads "With limits", with the reason.

Measured

I measured it, with the method and the date given. These are measurements of files and software, not tests with people reading. Most records are in my working notes, which aren't public yet, and most corpus results come from the version of 17 October 2026, which has been on GitHub since 30 October.

Known only from an abstract or another paper's account, or nobody has shown it yet. The open questions say what would settle each one.

Summary

Established

People with low vision differ in which way round they prefer text. In an online survey of 133 adults with low vision, 46% preferred white text on black and 39% black on white [1]. That is what they said, not how they read.

Established

For people with typical sight, dark text on light gives better proofreading and sharper fine detail, most of all for small text [2] [3]. An earlier study from the same lab, with 124 adults and low-contrast text, put the advantage down to the brighter screen rather than the polarity itself [4].

Established, with limits

Some people with low vision use colour inversion, and on charts it gives unpredictable results. In interviews with 11 people, one participant's bar chart didn't invert as he expected, and one chart still didn't comply with WCAG once a participant's filter had inverted it [5].

Established, with limits

In one small study, the main loss from inverting a chart was what its colours mean. Twelve people with typical sight read inverted charts with no significant difference in accuracy or speed from the originals, but rated the inverted colours far less like the originals than a designed dark version (1.4 against 4.4 out of 7) [6].

Established, with limits

Warm backgrounds gave faster reading than cool ones in 341 readers, with and without dyslexia, though white wasn't among the colours tested [7]. Colour doesn't treat dyslexia, and the systematic reviews of coloured overlays disagree [8] [9].

Established, with limits

Colour choice matters, and a second cue helps. In a crowded display shown for about a tenth of a second, people picked out a group more accurately when it differed in both colour and shape than in either alone [10], and a rainbow colour scale cost medical students accuracy against a diverging one [11]. Both studies were small.

Established, with limits

A colour scale can keep its colours on a dark page if its darkest colours still stand out from the page. With no key on the maps, 214 people, two thirds of them self-rated map experts, took darker as more in 95 to 97% of answers on a light page and 87 to 94% with the same colours on a dark page. A tool's dark version whose darkest colours nearly matched the page (1.2 to 1.8:1) cut that to about half, with a third unsure [12].

Measured

Adding themes to 299 public PDFs, the tool refused one broken file and left 297 of the other 298 drawing as before in their default colours in PDFium: 266 identically and 31 within one colour level. Eight themes added 4.3% to a page of plots.

Measured

Many PDFs fall short already. In 114 of the 298, some text was below the WCAG contrast minimum in the author's own colours, as the tool measures it. A theme the tool works out is held to the minimum anyway, so it lifts faint text rather than copying the problem.

Measured

When a theme swapped in a heavier line width or a different dash pattern, three PDF engines (PDFium, which is in Chrome, MuPDF and Poppler) drew it, so a theme could draw lines heavier. Most tools set line widths where a theme can't reach them, and the proposal keeps themes to colour for now.

Open

Inverting the colours in a document editor's preview turned a photo into a negative and flipped a figure that had its own dark version back to light. The test page used thesis material, so it isn't published, and the test is to be repeated on a page that can be.

Open

Nobody has tested themed PDFs with people reading them. I found no test of today's chart palettes with readers who have colour vision deficiency, and none that measures whether heavier lines help people with low vision.

Light text on dark helps some people with low vision, more often those whose eyes scatter light, as with cataract, but one study of children and teenagers found no clinical test that predicted who, and one test with cataract found only a slight gain. So readers have to be able to choose. For typical sight dark on light gives better proofreading and acuity, most of all for small text; the same lab found the advantage comes from the brighter screen rather than the polarity itself, in one study that used low-contrast text in a dark room. In a near-dark room light text on dark took longer to read at a glance; in bright light it didn't. So light stays the default, and dark is a choice. In the chart study each person did only a few trials in each polarity, so a single person's "better polarity" is a noisy measure; the paper itself found no effect of polarity on average. The claim that light text looks smeared on dark to people with astigmatism ("halation") appears in accessibility advice, for example from Level Access [27], which cites no study for it. The only controlled study I found that varied both astigmatism and polarity measured speed, not smearing. It was small, simulated the astigmatism with lenses and used 1990 displays. In a later small study, light squares among black and white noise were harder to count than dark ones, more so with strong blur from lenses, but the blur wasn't astigmatism and smearing wasn't measured [28].

None of these tested a PDF app's dark mode. They show that the recolouring people rely on gives unpredictable results on charts. Whether it makes charts harder to read hasn't been shown: the one study that measured it, in 12 people with typical sight, found no cost in accuracy or time. Prakash and others didn't test recolouring; their study is here for what it shows about reading charts with low vision. My own test of inversion in a document editor is under What I measured, and is still open.

Open: PDF apps that recolour a page themselves include Evince's night mode, Skim's lightness inversion, pdf.js in forced colours and Acrobat's Replace Document Colors (my search of 9 October 2026, not yet shown in screenshots). A PDF says which colour to paint, but not what a colour means. An app can tell text from a picture, but not whether a blue line is a data series, a highlight or a brand colour, nor whether a picture is a photo or a diagram. So a PDF app that offers a dark mode treats colours by rule: some invert every colour, some invert only lightness, some swap in their own colours.

The tints are offered for comfort and choice, and nobody with dyslexia, visual stress or low vision has tried them yet. Colour doesn't treat dyslexia. Visual stress is discomfort when reading, such as eye strain or headaches, and one review puts it at about one in five people with dyslexia [9]. I found no standard colours for the tints, and the ones used here were chosen for the project, in two sets that don't yet agree. They are paler than the tested colours, and this site's "turquoise" (#E3EEFA) has an OKLCH hue of 250, which is a pale blue, near the cool colours that read slowest in 2017; the tool's own turquoise (#D1F8F4) is a true pale turquoise. Black text reaches well over 7:1 on each tested colour shown. In one small study some readers with dyslexia chose low contrast as more restful [37]; the tints here keep text at 4.5:1 or more. Which tints to offer is one of the open questions.

The tests I found with readers who have colour vision deficiency are of maps, coded displays and ratings; I found none of today's chart palettes, such as viridis or Okabe and Ito's, with such readers. Simulations are what most checks rely on, and in one study 9 of 12 interviewees with the deficiency disputed them [56]. Most readers still take darker as more on a dark page, so a colour scale should keep its lightness order in every theme, with its darkest colours still visible against the page: a dark version whose darkest classes nearly vanished left about half reading it that way and a third unsure [12]. Keeping darker as more in every theme is my inference from this. On a dark background, scales that look like one colour fading into the background were read faster with lighter as more, and the authors advise scales that don't look like that on any background [59]. WCAG 1.4.1 asks that colour is not the only way information is given [63].

What journals ask of authors varies. PLOS's figure guidelines say "Preparation and final quality of figures is the responsibility of the author" and "PLOS does not provide graphics services"; they take TIFF or EPS only, and say nothing about colour vision deficiency [97]. A figure supplied as TIFF is a picture: its text can't be searched or read aloud, and it can't follow a theme. Settling a figure's colours, second cues and text size in a shared pipeline, and checking them when the document is built, as pdf-themes checks text contrast, would make them the default rather than each author's job. That last step is my inference from these studies; none of them tested such a pipeline.

Open questions

What would settle them

  • Do checked themes help people with low vision read charts better than inverting does? A task study, measuring accuracy and time as well as preference, with the author's themes against the reader's inversion.
  • Should a theme be allowed to change line width and dash patterns? People with low vision have asked for bolder grids and ticks, but I found no measurement of the gain. A study of chart reading with heavier lines, and a decision in the specification (open question 2 of the proposal, which asks about line width; dash patterns would join it).
  • Which tints? The tested colours or paler ones, whether to add orange, and whether a standard should fix the colours or only name them. Readers with dyslexia or visual stress trying both sets would settle it.
  • Which chart colours work for readers with colour vision deficiency, in each theme? The studies with such readers are of maps, coded displays and ratings of charts, and the checks most tools use are simulations. A chart-reading study with them, in light and dark.
  • How big should a figure's text be? I found print guidance, a study of map labels with young students, and a think-aloud study in which some older adults found some chart text too small. A study of label size in charts, at the size they are printed.
  • Does a dark theme cost people with astigmatism? The one controlled test I found is small and old. A study with natural astigmatism on today's screens.
  • Must a theme the tool works out pass the contrast check outright, or only be no worse than the author's own colours? In the run of 17 October, 114 of the 298 files already fell short in their own colours, and 205 of the 225 worked-out themes left out for contrast came from those files. A decision in the specification.
  • Should the check also cover lines and shapes at 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11), and colour vision deficiency? Today lines and shapes under 3:1 are listed for the author but don't decide the mark. A decision in the specification, and a check that can tell which shapes carry meaning.
  • Why do 31 files differ from their originals by one colour level? Sorting them by cause is the next stage of the run over public PDFs.
  • Do the results hold in Acrobat, Apple Preview and pdf.js, and for files over 40 pages? The comparisons so far were made in PDFium, MuPDF and Poppler, on files of up to 40 pages.
  • What do the contrast check's two known limits change? Text in a figure drawn smaller than it was set can be held to the large-text minimum, and text across two backgrounds is measured against the one it contrasts with best. Both need a fix, tests and a fresh run over the set, which would change the counts under What I measured.
  • Will PDF readers adopt it? A standard name from the PDF Association, and themes shown by one widely used PDF reader, such as pdf.js.

How this was gathered

Searches, checks and what was left out

  • The searches were made on 9 and 24 October 2026 in PubMed, Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, arXiv and the web, for the best direct evidence on each point. A second search on 24 October looked for studies missing from the first, in six areas: colour vision deficiency, marks and lines, text and labels, tints, polarity, and readers with cognitive differences. A third, also on 24 October, looked for audits of figures as journals publish them and for what journals ask of authors; its sources were read once, in full or as abstracts as marked, with their DOIs checked against Crossref. They are not a systematic review, so "I found no study" means none turned up in these searches. Studies from the second search that bear on a claim are listed whether they support it or not.
  • Statements from 27 sources were checked against the source itself, then checked again, in a separate pass, for errors in that check. The 28 sources from my earlier notes were re-checked once, on 24 October, against their full text where it could be reached. The 48 studies from the second search were each found in one pass and checked in another. Every DOI was checked against Crossref, or against DataCite for the one thesis registered there. Statements were corrected where the source says something different.
  • The Strength column marks each study. Established: read in full, peer reviewed, with 30 people or more (or a systematic review, or a standard's own text), and stated within its limits. With limits: read in full, but small, a thesis, a preprint or a smaller venue, crowd-sourced with caveats, or resting on the authors' inference; the reason is given. Open: known only from an abstract or another paper's account.
  • Left out after checking: two percentages that my earlier notes attributed to a conference abstract on labels and legends appear in no readable source; the abstract of the ADHD study says ADHD affected accuracy, which its results don't show; a study that Rello and Bigham (2017) cite as repeating the cream result with 92 people didn't find it, so it isn't cited as a repeat; and two studies of text in charts, on irrelevant details in bar graphs and on annotated line charts, bear on no claim here.

Found and not tabled

The second search also found these. They bear on the claims above but aren't in the tables.

  • Eperjesi, Fowler and Evans 2004 [108] (Open: abstract only). In 12 people with macular degeneration, ten coloured overlays made no significant difference to reading speed against a clear one.
  • D'Ath 2008 [109] (With limits: a doctoral thesis). Of 40 office workers, 39 preferred a tinted screen to white, but their reading and task scores didn't change significantly, and in a pilot with three groups of 10 people with low vision, the colour each chose didn't speed their reading significantly. The tints were also dimmer than white.
  • Jiménez et al. 2020 [110] (With limits: 20 people). In young adults with typical sight, the colours of text and background made no significant difference to reading speed, though dark on light was faster overall; white text on yellow, with almost no contrast, was rated least legible.
  • Westheimer et al. 2003 [75] (Open: abstract only). In 106 patients aged 20 to 88, letter acuity was on average about 4% better with white letters on black, and more so the older the patient. Single letters, not reading.
  • Lillo, Álvaro and Moreira 2014 [52] (Open: abstract only). Of three tools that simulate colour vision deficiency, checked against 10 people with full red-green colour blindness, only Vischeck appeared accurate; Coblis didn't. Angerbauer and others used Coblis for other forms of the deficiency. The tools may have changed since.
  • Chen et al. 2026 [111] (Open: abstract only). College students, 33 with colour vision deficiency and 44 without, did identification and comparison tasks on maps recoloured by the authors' method, which picks accessible colours from a painting or photograph, and the authors report that such maps are easily readable for both groups. The abstract gives no figures for accuracy or speed, and no comparison with other colours.
  • Wei et al. 2020 [112] (With limits: 20 people; the authors' version). When 20 students judged scatterplots seen for a quarter of a second, enlarging a plot with its points made it look less numerous and more correlated, and shrinking it did the reverse. Changing the size of the points only partly corrected this, and differently for each judgement; value reading wasn't tested.
  • Okan, Galesic and Garcia-Retamero 2016 [113] (With limits: read in the authors' manuscript). In two eye-tracking experiments, with 48 and then 51 adults aged 18 to 38, some health graphs were made so that the title, an axis label or the scale went against what the graph's shape suggested, such as an upside-down scale. People who scored lower on a test of graph reading more often went by the shape and answered wrongly, and in the second experiment most graphs with an unusual scale were answered correctly less than half the time, even by those who scored higher.
  • Galesic, Garcia-Retamero and Gigerenzer 2009 [114] (Established). In 59 adults aged 62 to 77 and 112 students in Berlin, adding an icon array (1,000 circles, those affected in black) to the numbers raised the share who got the size of a risk reduction right, most for those whose numeracy was low for their group, with no significant difference between the age groups. Low numeracy meant the lower half of each group, not a disability.
  • Tao, Yuan and Qu 2019 [115] (Open: abstract only). In 36 older and 36 young adults reading made-up personal health records, tables and line graphs with added visual cues took less time and gave fewer errors than plain versions, and a line graph with a coloured background was the format both groups preferred. Performance differed by age, but the abstract doesn't say which way, or what the other cues were.
  • Wood et al. 2026 [116] (With limits: 8 people, interviews; the authors' manuscript). In interviews and a week's diary, 4 adults with aphasia after a stroke and 4 with developmental language disorder described the charts and maps they met in daily life; many named colour coding as something that made a display usable, and one said colours came to them more quickly than names. Chart reading wasn't measured, and the authors weren't always sure which findings were particular to language disability.
